You might not need a rack at all. It's a good idea when you've got rack-mount gear, or enough devices and cables to make it worthwhile. Otherwise, a solid shelf works fine.

## Options

| Option | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shelving** | Mini PCs, towers, small NAS devices | Cable tidiness, and weight limits |
| **10-inch mini rack** | Mini PCs, compact switches, Raspberry Pis | Not all equipment fits. Many use 3D-printed mounts |
| **19-inch wall-mount or small open-frame rack** | Network gear, patch panels | Weight limits and wall strength |
| **19-inch 4-post rack** | Rack-mount servers | Needs depth, and takes up floor space |
| **Enclosed cabinet** | Keeping out dust, kids and pets, and some noise | Heat. Needs ventilation |
| **Sound-dampened cabinet** | Keeping loud gear in shared rooms | Very expensive new. Must have airflow |

A 19-inch rack is the standard for mounting equipment, measured by the width of each device's front panel. Height is measured in rack units (U), where 1U is 1.75 inches (44.45mm). Full-size racks are usually 42U, but many labs fit into 12U to 24U.

## What to check before you buy

- **Depth:** many servers are over 700mm (28 inches) deep, and rails and cables need further room. This is the most common mistake people make when buying racks. Shallow network racks won't fit most servers.
- **Post count:** two-post racks are fine for network gear, but heavy servers need four posts.
- **Weight:** a loaded rack is heavy. Put the heaviest gear at the bottom, and check that your floor, especially a loft floor, can handle it. Anchor tall racks so they can't tip.
- **Airflow:** servers pull cool air in from the front and push hot air out of the back. Leave space, and don't back up against a wall.
- **Access:** you need to be able to get to the back, so leave room, or use casters.
- **Rails:** match them to your servers. They're usually not universal.
- **Doors:** a locked door keeps children and curious pets out. Mesh ones help with airflow.

## Silent racks

These range from a basic rack with [acoustic material](/housing/noise/#pc-and-rack-acoustic-absorption-kits) to purpose-built cabinets that hide nearly all noise. Purpose-built models are expensive new, but they turn up used. Reducing the noise from your kit is often cheaper. See [Noise](/housing/noise/).

## Lack Racks

The IKEA LACK side table happens to be about 19 inches wide between its legs, which led to the famous [Lack Rack](https://wiki.eth0.nl/index.php/LackRack). It's a cheap, fun choice for light network gear, but don't trust it with heavy servers unless you reinforce it.

## Custom racks

Wooden frames, 3D-printed mounts, and even Lego have all been used in homelabs. If you build your own, plan for weight, airflow and electrical safety.
